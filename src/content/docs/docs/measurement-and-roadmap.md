## Current evidence

This is an implemented presentation prototype. No user study, live acquisition campaign, conversion experiment, or customer outcome is reported here. Analytics are not connected.

Build and browser checks establish technical behaviour; they do not establish that customers understand, prefer, or would buy the product.

## Proposed usability study

Start with a small qualitative round spanning first-time and existing homeowners. Include phone and desktop sessions and participants with different accessibility needs. Use observations to find problems before deciding whether a larger quantitative test is useful.

Give participants these tasks without pointing out the intended controls:

1. Describe who this product appears to be for and what it offers.
2. Find the difference between protection for the building and the belongings inside it.
3. Explain the proposed next step and what information they expect to need.
4. Find an answer to a question about claims.
5. Select the quote action and explain what this prototype can actually do.

For portfolio reviewers, add a separate task: locate the product rationale and explain one tradeoff made in the first version. Keep reviewer behaviour separate from homeowner behaviour.

Record task success, misunderstandings, unexpected paths, and questions. Obtain consent before recording sessions or collecting identifiable information.

## Proposed measures

| Measure | Definition | What it helps us learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proposition comprehension | Participants who correctly describe the audience and purpose ÷ participants asked | Whether the first impression is clear |
| Cover comprehension | Participants who distinguish the building and belongings categories ÷ participants asked | Whether the explanation is useful |
| Next-step discovery | Participants who find the quote action without a prompt ÷ participants assigned the task | Whether the primary action is discoverable |
| Quote-action rate, in a future measured test | Eligible landing-page sessions with a quote-button click ÷ eligible landing-page sessions | Directional interest, subject to traffic quality and visitor intent |
| Prototype-boundary comprehension | Participants who recognise that no real quote or policy is available ÷ participants asked | Whether the concept is being understood honestly |
| Reviewer rationale discovery | Reviewers who find and explain a documented decision ÷ reviewers asked | Whether the portfolio supports evaluation |

Set numerical targets after an initial baseline and an agreed research design. Do not treat prototype clicks as quote completions, sales, or validated willingness to pay.

## Possible future instrumentation

If a measured test is justified, distinguish quote-button location, FAQ engagement, and documentation visits using minimal event properties. Exclude personal information and establish the appropriate consent approach before connecting analytics. No such events are sent by this version.

## Roadmap by decision

| Stage | Work | Decision it should support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Validate the explanation | Run comprehension and navigation tasks | Is the proposition clear enough to develop further? |
| 2. Improve the page | Revise the most consequential observed friction and retest | Did the changes address the problem? |
| 3. Define a specific market | Investigate terminology, eligibility, product requirements, and operating constraints with appropriate specialists | Can this become a credible product proposition? |
| 4. Prototype the quote journey | Explore questions, progress, error recovery, and consent | Can people move through the intended next step? |
| 5. Consider a real pilot | Establish operational, technical, product, and measurement readiness | Is a pilot justified and supportable? |

## Keep a learning log

For each research round, add the date, method, participant profile, observation, decision, and remaining uncertainty. Link important decisions back to the evidence. A change in direction is useful product evidence when its reasoning is clear.
