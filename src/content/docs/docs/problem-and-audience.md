## The problem to investigate

Home insurance asks people to make an important decision about an unfamiliar product. The working assumption for Hearthline is that visitors need a clear starting point: what could be protected, why the provider feels credible, and what they would need to do next.

This is a problem framing for discovery, rather than a finding from completed research.

## Primary audience

The initial audience is an owner-occupier considering protection for their home and possessions. Two useful research segments are first-time homeowners learning the categories and existing homeowners reassessing their cover.

| Segment hypothesis | Possible need | What would validate it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A first-time homeowner | Understand the difference between the building and its contents | Interviews and a category-comprehension task |
| A homeowner reviewing existing cover | Identify relevant protection and a clear next step | Observation of a real comparison journey |
| A visitor using a phone | Scan the proposition and find useful information without friction | Mobile usability sessions |

These segments are intentionally broad. Geography, property eligibility, accessibility needs, and buying triggers require further discovery before a real product could be defined.

## Job to be done

**When I am looking for home insurance, I want to understand what protection could mean for my home and belongings, so I can decide whether taking the next step is worth my time.**

This framing prioritises comprehension and confidence before collecting information.

## Assumed questions along the journey

1. Is this product for someone like me?
2. What does it help me protect?
3. What would the process involve?
4. What happens when something goes wrong?
5. Where can I go next?

These questions inform the page order. They are also prompts to test, rather than a claim that every visitor follows the same sequence.

## Research to run

- Interview homeowners at different points in the buying journey. Ask about their last decision and the information they actually used.
- Observe people comparing product pages. Note unfamiliar terms, missing information, and the signals they use to judge credibility.
- Test this prototype on phones and desktops, including keyboard and assistive-technology use.
- Ask participants to explain the cover categories in their own words before prompting them with the intended meanings.

Avoid asking only whether people like the design. Record whether they understand the offer, notice the next step, and recognise that the site is a concept.

## Open questions

- Does plain language improve understanding, or leave participants wanting more policy detail?
- Which homeowner situations should a future version prioritise?
- Does the established visual identity help people understand the proposition without implying a real insurer?
- Which market should define the terminology, eligibility, and eventual policy detail?
